How to Forgive Someone Who Hurt You in Islam — Even When You Don't Want To

You know Islam says to forgive. You know the ayat. You know the hadith. And you still cannot do it. This is not a character flaw — this is what genuine hurt feels like. Here is what Islam actually says about the path through it.

Published: July 21, 2026 · Updated: July 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Let's start with the truth most Islamic content skips over.

You know who hurt you. You know what they did. And you have been told — by family, by imams, by that voice in your own head — that you need to forgive them. Maybe you've tried. Maybe you said the words. Maybe you made dua for them once, teeth clenched, and hoped that counted.

But the resentment is still there. The anger is still there. And underneath both of those — the hurt, which is the thing nobody talks about.

So now you carry two weights instead of one. The original wound. And the guilt of not being able to forgive it.

This post is not going to tell you to "just forgive and move on." It is going to tell you what forgiveness in Islam actually means, why you might be making it harder than it needs to be, and how to genuinely move toward it — even when every part of you is not ready.

What Forgiveness in Islam Actually Is — And What It Is Not

The reason most people cannot forgive is that they have the wrong definition. They are trying to do something Islam never asked them to do.

Forgiveness is not saying what happened was okay

The Arabic word the Quran uses most often for forgiveness is 'afw — which means to pardon, to wipe away, to let go of a right you hold. It is not the same as saying the harm was acceptable. It is not minimising what was done to you. It is not agreeing that they were right.

When you forgive someone in the Islamic sense, you are not erasing the wrong. You are choosing to release your claim over it — and handing it to Allah instead.

That distinction changes everything. You do not have to pretend they did not hurt you. You do not have to say it was fine. You only have to decide you are no longer going to carry it yourself.

Forgiveness is not the same as reconciliation

Islam does not require you to resume a relationship with someone who harmed you. You can forgive someone completely — in your heart, before Allah — and still maintain healthy boundaries. You can forgive a parent who abused you without having Sunday dinner with them. You can forgive a friend who betrayed you without trusting them again.

Reconciliation may or may not follow forgiveness. That depends on the situation, the person, and whether it is safe. But forgiveness itself is internal. It happens between you and Allah. The other person does not even need to know.

Forgiveness is not forgetting

"Forgive and forget" is a cultural phrase, not an Islamic one. Memory is not the enemy of forgiveness — unprocessed resentment is. You can remember clearly what someone did to you, carry appropriate caution about them, and still have genuinely forgiven them. The two are not in conflict.

Why You Cannot Forgive Yet — And Why That Does Not Make You a Bad Muslim

The wound is still fresh

There is a timeline to healing that cannot be rushed by guilt or willpower. If someone broke your arm yesterday, you would not be expected to run a race today. Emotional wounds are not different — they just do not have a cast you can see.

The fact that you cannot forgive yet does not mean you never will. It may simply mean you are in the middle of the injury, not at the end of it. That is a normal human experience, not a spiritual failure.

Nobody has heard you

One of the reasons resentment stays is that the hurt was never acknowledged — not by the person who caused it, not by the people around you, sometimes not even by yourself. When we rush to "you should forgive" before "that was genuinely wrong and it hurt you," we skip the validation that is part of healing.

You do not need the person who hurt you to acknowledge it. But you need to acknowledge it to yourself. What happened was real. It was wrong. It affected you. All of that can be true at the same time as "I am working toward forgiving them."

You think forgiving them means letting them off the hook

This is the most common misunderstanding. People resist forgiveness because they feel it would be an injustice — that the person would walk away without consequence, that the hurt would be erased, that they "get away with it."

But forgiveness does not erase accountability. The person who hurt you still carries that deed before Allah. The person who hurt you will still face what they did on the Day of Judgment — unless they have made sincere tawbah, in which case Allah has already dealt with it in His infinite justice.

When you choose to forgive, you are not wiping their slate. You are releasing the grip that their deed has on your heart. Those are completely different things.

What Allah Says About the Person Who Forgives

"The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is with Allah. Indeed, He does not like wrongdoers."
Surah Ash-Shura, 42:40

Read that carefully: whoever pardons — their reward is with Allah. Not a general reward. A reward that Allah Himself holds. That is the weight this act carries in the sight of Allah.

And then the ayah that should settle the question entirely:

"And whoever is patient and forgives — indeed, that is of the matters worthy of determination."
Surah Ash-Shura, 42:43

Min 'azm il-umoor — matters of strong resolve. The Quran places forgiving someone who wronged you among the acts that require genuine spiritual strength and courage. It is not weakness. It is not naivety. It is one of the highest acts a Muslim can perform.

And there is this, which Abu Bakr (RA) lived so literally that it became part of our Quran:

"...Let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you? And Allah is Forgiving and Merciful."
Surah An-Nur, 24:22

After the slander campaign against Aisha (RA), Mistah ibn Uthatha — a relative of Abu Bakr (RA) who had participated in spreading it — had his financial support cut off by Abu Bakr in understandable grief and anger. Then this ayah was revealed. Abu Bakr (RA) said: "By Allah, I love that Allah should forgive me" — and he immediately restored Mistah's support. (Sahih al-Bukhari, 4141)

Abu Bakr (RA), the closest companion of the Prophet ﷺ, was angry. He had every right to be. And he forgave anyway — not because the hurt was small, but because the promise of Allah's forgiveness was larger.

The Prophet ﷺ confirmed what this act returns:

"No one forgives another except that Allah increases his honour."
Sahih Muslim, 2588

Not "might increase." Increases. The honour that comes from forgiving someone who wronged you is guaranteed by Allah.

What Unforgiveness Actually Does to You

This needs to be said plainly because it is rarely spoken about in Islamic spaces: harbouring resentment harms the person who holds it more than the person it is directed at.

The one who hurt you has moved on with their life. The resentment lives in your chest, not theirs. It sits with you during salah. It wakes you up at 3am. It colours your perception of new relationships. It takes up space that could be filled with peace.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

"Do not envy one another, do not hate one another, do not turn your backs on one another, and do not undercut one another. Be, O servants of Allah, brothers."
Sahih Muslim, 2563

And He ﷺ specifically said:

"It is not permissible for a Muslim to boycott his brother for more than three days, each of them turning away from the other when they meet. The better of the two is the one who is first to greet the other."
Sahih al-Bukhari, 6076 · Sahih Muslim, 2560

The one who breaks the resentment first is described as the better of the two. Not the weaker. Not the one who surrendered. The better.

Forgiveness is not a gift you give the person who hurt you. It is something you do for yourself — and for your relationship with Allah.

How to Actually Move Toward Forgiveness

Name What Happened — Out Loud

Before you can release something, you have to hold it clearly. Write it down if that is easier. Name specifically what they did, how it made you feel, and what it cost you. Do not minimise it and do not catastrophise it — just describe it accurately.

This is not about rehearsing the grievance. It is about acknowledging it so it stops living in the unnamed dark. A wound you can name is a wound you can begin to treat.

Understand the Difference Between the Person and the Deed

The person who hurt you is still a human being — a flawed, struggling, probably in-pain human being who acted badly. That does not make what they did acceptable. But it does make them human.

Allah says:

"Take what is given freely, enjoin what is good, and turn away from the ignorant."
Surah Al-A'raf, 7:199

"The ignorant" here — al-jahileen — does not necessarily mean uneducated. It refers to those who act from a place of heedlessness, weakness, or ego. Most people who hurt others are not monsters. They are people acting from their own damage, their own fear, their own blindness.

Understanding this does not erase accountability. It does make forgiveness more possible.

Make One Small Dua for Them

This is the hardest step and the most effective one.

You do not need to pray that they are blessed with everything they ever wanted. Start with one sentence: "Ya Allah, guide them." That is it. One honest sentence. Because guidance — being brought closer to Allah — is the best thing that could happen to any person, and asking it for someone who hurt you is an act of genuine generosity.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

"Show mercy to those on earth and the One in the heavens will show mercy to you."
Jami' at-Tirmidhi, 1924

When you make dua for the person who hurt you — even a reluctant, single-sentence dua — something shifts. Not immediately. But it shifts. Because you have chosen, for one moment, their wellbeing over your grievance.

Give the Justice Question to Allah

The reason we hold onto resentment is often because we are afraid they will never face any consequence. That justice will not arrive. That the score will stay unbalanced forever.

Islam's answer to this is the most important thing you can internalise: you are not the keeper of justice. Allah is.

"And whoever is patient and forgives — indeed, that is of the matters worthy of determination."
Surah Ash-Shura, 42:43

When you forgive, you are not erasing the account. You are handing it to the only Judge who sees every detail — including the parts you do not know about, including what that person was going through, including every nuance of what happened. Allah's justice is infinitely more precise than anything you or anyone else could administer.

Releasing the justice question to Allah is not giving up. It is trusting the only One who can actually handle it correctly.

Set the Boundary. Then Release the Grip.

Forgiveness and boundaries can and should coexist. After you have done the internal work — named the hurt, made the dua, given the justice to Allah — you are allowed to decide how much access this person has to your life going forward.

That boundary is not the same as the resentment. The resentment is the tightness in your chest when you think of them. The boundary is the practical decision about how much contact is healthy. One can dissolve while the other remains in place. In fact, that is often the healthiest outcome: you genuinely wish them no harm, you genuinely feel no hatred, and you genuinely choose not to give them close access to your life.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a sin to not forgive someone?

Not forgiving is not explicitly listed as a sin in the Quran or Sunnah in the way that certain actions are. However, Allah praises those who forgive and promises them elevated honour and His own forgiveness in return. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever does not show mercy will not be shown mercy." (Sahih al-Bukhari, 5997 · Sahih Muslim, 2318). The emphasis throughout the Quran and Sunnah is on forgiveness being deeply rewarded — not that its absence is punished the way other sins are. But the spiritual cost of holding on to resentment, as described above, is real.

What if they never apologised and never will?

Your forgiveness does not require their apology. Forgiveness is between you and Allah — it is an internal act of releasing a claim. The Prophet ﷺ forgave the people of Makkah at the Conquest — people who had tortured his companions, killed his loved ones, persecuted the early Muslims for thirteen years — without waiting for each of them to individually apologise. His forgiveness was unconditional. Yours does not need to be that sweeping, but its principle is the same: you choose to release for your own sake and Allah's reward, not because they earned it.

Does forgiving someone mean I have to trust them again?

No. Trust is rebuilt through consistent behaviour over time. Forgiveness is a one-time act of the heart. You can forgive someone and still keep a healthy distance from them. You can forgive a person who lied to you without giving them access to sensitive information again. Forgiving and trusting are two separate things — Islam requires the first in certain circumstances, never the second simply as a consequence of it.

What if I forgive them and then feel the anger again?

That is normal, and it does not undo the forgiveness. Emotions are not switches. You may choose to forgive and then feel waves of hurt or anger for weeks or months afterward. Each time, return to the intention. Renew the dua. That returning is itself the act. The scholars explain that 'afw (pardon) is primarily a decision of the will, not a feeling — and decisions can be made even when feelings lag behind.

Is there a specific dua for helping you forgive?

The Prophet ﷺ taught us to seek Allah's help in all matters: "O Allah, I ask You for guidance, piety, chastity and self-sufficiency." (Sahih Muslim, 2721). You can also make the dua of Yusuf (AS) after his brothers came to him — his response to the greatest betrayal in the Quran was: "No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful." (Surah Yusuf, 12:92). Let his words be yours. Read the full surah in the RuhVerse Quran reader with translation — it is the most complete account of a human being moving through betrayal, patience, and forgiveness that exists.

What if what they did was genuinely very serious — abuse, betrayal, a crime?

This post is written for hurt in the broad range of human relationships. For serious harm — abuse, assault, crimes — forgiveness in the Islamic sense still means releasing the resentment for your own spiritual health. It does not mean dropping legal consequences, returning to danger, or pretending the harm was acceptable. In serious cases, please speak to a knowledgeable scholar about your specific situation and consider professional support for the trauma itself. Forgiveness and justice are not in conflict — both can be sought simultaneously.

Forgiveness Is for You, Not for Them

The person who hurt you may never know you forgave them. They may never deserve it. They may never change.

None of that is the point.

Allah says He increases the honour of the one who forgives. The Prophet ﷺ lived this so completely that at the greatest moment of his worldly power — standing over the people who had persecuted him for decades — he asked them: "What do you think I will do with you?" They said: "A noble brother and the son of a noble brother." And he said: "Go — you are free." (Sirah Ibn Hisham, Vol. 4)

He did not need an apology. He did not need them to earn it. He gave it because that is who he was — and because it was the right thing, regardless of what they had done.

You are not the Prophet ﷺ. But you are trying to walk his path. And forgiving someone who hurt you — even imperfectly, even slowly, even with dua through clenched teeth — is one of the most Prophetic things a person can do.

Start small. Make one dua for them tonight. Hand the justice to Allah.

The rest follows.
