I Have Intrusive Thoughts During Prayer — Am I a Bad Muslim? (What Waswas Actually Is)

A thought entered your mind during salah that horrified you. You don't know where it came from. You don't want it. And now you're convinced something is deeply wrong with your faith.

Published: August 1, 2026 · Updated: August 1, 2026 · 8 min read

You were standing in salah.

Maybe it was Fajr, still dark outside, the house quiet. Maybe it was Dhuhr, wedged between two meetings. You started reciting, and then — without any warning, without any invitation — a thought arrived. Blasphemous. Disturbing. Completely unwanted.

It might have been a doubt about Allah. A vulgar image. An offensive thought about the Prophet ﷺ. Something so out of character for who you are that just writing this, you feel the same wave of horror you felt in that moment.

And now you're carrying questions you're too ashamed to say out loud. Did that invalidate my salah? Does this mean I'm a hypocrite? Does it mean I don't actually believe? Why would a thought like that enter the mind of a believer? Why does it keep happening?

You need to hear this clearly and without softening: you are not a bad Muslim. You are not a hypocrite. What happened to you has a name, a cause, and a very clear Islamic response — and it has been happening to believers since the time of the Prophet ﷺ.

This post will tell you exactly what waswas is, what it means, and what to do about it.

What Actually Just Happened in Your Prayer

Waswas Is a Quranic Concept — Not a Modern Problem

The word waswas appears in the Quran itself. Allah dedicated the final surah — Surah An-Nas — specifically to this phenomenon:

"Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind, the Sovereign of mankind, the God of mankind — from the evil of the retreating whisperer, who whispers into the hearts of mankind — from among jinn and men."
Surah An-Nas, 114:1–6

Read that carefully. Allah taught us a dua specifically for a whisperer who plants thoughts into human hearts. This is in the Quran. It was revealed to the Prophet ﷺ and to every Muslim who came after him as a daily protection and as a confirmation: this experience is real, it is external, and it is something that happens to believers.

The waswas — the whisperer — retreats when Allah is remembered and returns when the remembrance stops. Prayer is the highest act of remembrance. Of course the attack intensifies then.

The fact that you are being whispered to during salah is not evidence that something is wrong with your faith. It is evidence that you are praying.

The Thought Arriving Is Not the Sin

This is the most important thing you need to understand, and it comes directly from the Prophet ﷺ:

"Allah has forgiven my Ummah for whatever crosses their minds, as long as they do not act upon it or speak of it."
Sahih al-Bukhari, 5269 · Sahih Muslim, 127

A thought crossing the mind — even a terrible one — is not a sin. It is not chosen. It is not yours in the way a spoken word or a deliberate action is yours. The thought arrives uninvited. The sin, if there were one, would be in embracing it, acting on it, or speaking it.

You did none of those things. You were horrified. You pushed it away. That response — that revulsion — is not the response of someone who believes those thoughts. It is the response of someone who absolutely does not.

The More Horrified You Are, the Stronger Your Faith

This is where the most important hadith on this subject arrives — and it should change everything about how you feel right now.

"Some of the Companions of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came to him and said: 'We find in ourselves thoughts that are too terrible to speak of.' He ﷺ said: 'Are you really suffering from that?' They said: 'Yes.' He ﷺ said: 'That is a clear sign of faith.'"
Sahih Muslim, 132

The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ. The best generation of Muslims to ever live. They came to him describing thoughts so disturbing they couldn't even say them aloud — and his response was not concern, not rebuke, not instruction for repentance. It was: that is a clear sign of faith.

Why? Because the horror you feel at the thought is evidence of your belief. A person who did not believe would not be disturbed. The waswas attacks specifically where there is something worth attacking. Shaitan does not bother whispering to the person whose heart is already empty of faith.

Your horror is your faith speaking. The thought is not.

The Ayah That Speaks Directly to This Moment

"Allah does not burden a soul beyond that it can bear."
Surah Al-Baqarah, 2:286

This ayah is about more than just hardship. Scholars have explained that it applies to accountability itself — Allah does not hold a soul accountable for what it cannot control. An uninvited thought is not within your control. The waswas comes without your permission. You are not accountable for its arrival.

What you are responsible for is the response — and your response, the fact that you are reading this, that you felt horror rather than agreement, that you want to understand and resist this, is already the right response.

Allah also says:

"And if an evil suggestion comes to you from Shaitan, seek refuge in Allah. Indeed, He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."
Surah Al-A'raf, 7:200

The instruction is simple and immediate: seek refuge. Not analysis, not endless reflection, not interrogating every thought to check whether it was "really" from you or from Shaitan. Seek refuge. Move forward. The thought does not deserve more attention than that.

When Waswas Becomes Something More

Most Muslims experience waswas as an occasional disturbance — thoughts that arrive during prayer, are pushed away, and leave. This is normal and universal.

But for some Muslims, the experience is more severe. The thoughts come constantly. They repeat wudu five or six times because they can't be sure it was valid. They repeat salah from the beginning because a single syllable felt wrong. They avoid prayer altogether because the distress of being in it is unbearable. They spend hours after prayer replaying every moment in a cycle of doubt that never fully resolves.

This pattern has a name in Islamic scholarship — waswas al-qahri, or overwhelming waswas — and it closely mirrors what clinical psychology calls Scrupulosity OCD: OCD centered on religious themes.

If your experience looks more like the second description than the first, two things are true simultaneously:

Islamically: The overwhelming doubt is itself a product of the waswas, not a real indicator of your prayer's validity. The Prophet ﷺ said: "If any of you gets a waswas during salah, he should seek refuge with Allah from Shaitan and blow dryly to his left three times." (Sahih al-Bukhari, 3282). Scholars consistently rule that a person with severe waswas should build on certainty — if you knew your wudu was valid before prayer, it is valid. If you intended a specific number of rakats, that is what you prayed. Doubt that arrives during worship from this kind of waswas is dismissed, not investigated.

Practically: Severe and intrusive religious OCD responds well to a specific therapy called ERP (Exposure and Response Prevention), and seeking that help is entirely consistent with Islamic values. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Make use of medical treatment, for Allah has not made a disease without appointing a remedy for it." (Abu Dawud, 3855). Seeking help for a mental health condition is as Islamic as seeking help for a physical one.

If what you are experiencing is severe, please consider speaking to a Muslim therapist who understands both the clinical and spiritual dimensions of this experience.

What to Actually Do — Step by Step

Do Not Engage the Thought

The worst thing you can do with a waswas is argue with it. Analysing it, trying to determine whether it came from you or Shaitan, asking yourself "do I actually believe this?" — this gives the thought oxygen. It grows in direct proportion to the attention you give it.

The Islamic instruction is not to investigate the thought. It is to dismiss it. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

"Seek refuge with Allah and stop [entertaining those thoughts]."
Sahih al-Bukhari, 3276 · Sahih Muslim, 134

Stop. Not examine. Not resolve. Stop.

Say A'udhu Billahi Min Al-Shaitan Ir-Rajim — and Mean It

The moment a disturbing thought enters during prayer or outside it, say:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ
A'udhu billahi min al-shaitan ir-rajim
"I seek refuge in Allah from Shaitan, the accursed."

This is not a superstition. It is the Prophetic instruction — the act of turning away from the whisper and toward Allah in a single breath. Use the RuhVerse Qibla tool to centre yourself physically if you need grounding, and use the Quran reader to find Surah An-Nas and Al-Falaq — the two surahs revealed specifically as protection from this.

Keep Praying — Do Not Start Over

One of the ways severe waswas takes hold is by convincing you to restart your salah every time a thought comes. This is the trap. If you restart, the waswas wins — and it will send another thought in the next attempt too.

Unless you have a genuine, certain reason to doubt a pillar of your prayer — you missed a rakat, you are certain your wudu broke — finish what you started. The thought that entered did not invalidate your salah. The prayer is accepted. Complete it.

Build Your Armour Outside of Prayer

The waswas attacks most effectively when the protective layer of dhikr is thin. If prayer is the only time you are connected to Allah, the contrast when you enter it is stark — and the whisper rushes in to fill the silence.

The morning and evening adhkar (remembrance) prescribed by the Prophet ﷺ are specifically designed to build a protective layer around your day. Ayat al-Kursi (Quran 2:255) recited after every fard prayer, the three Quls before sleeping, Bismillah before entering any space — these are not rituals of routine. They are daily maintenance of the spiritual barrier that waswas erodes.

Open the RuhVerse Quran reader and save Ayat al-Kursi and the three Quls as your daily practice. The protection is cumulative. The more consistently you build it, the less ground the waswas finds.

Track the Pattern, Not the Thought

If you experience this regularly, start noticing when it is worse — not what the thoughts are, but when they come. Stressed? Sleep-deprived? Isolated? Spiritually dry from skipping dhikr? The waswas is opportunistic. It attacks in the gaps. Understanding your own vulnerability pattern lets you protect those moments before they become attacks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did my salah count even though I had those thoughts?

Yes. A thought passing through the mind does not invalidate salah. Your salah was broken only if you deliberately spoke words outside the prayer, laughed audibly, or lost wudu through a physical cause. An involuntary thought — however disturbing — is not among the invalidators of salah. Your prayer counted. Make no qada for it.

Do I need to make tawbah for having these thoughts?

No. Tawbah is for sins — for chosen actions or words. An unwanted intrusive thought that you rejected is not a sin. Scholars are unanimous on this. Making tawbah for a thought that was not yours to begin with treats it as though you were responsible for it, which both the Quran and the hadith explicitly say you are not.

Why does this happen more when I'm trying to pray than at any other time?

Because prayer is the highest act of connection to Allah, and waswas specifically targets acts of worship. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Shaitan comes to one of you in his prayer and says: 'Remember this, remember that' — to keep him from knowing how many rakats he has prayed." (Sahih al-Bukhari, 1232). The intensity during prayer is not a sign you are failing — it is a sign you are doing the most important thing.

What if the thought keeps coming back no matter how many times I push it away?

Push it away once, seek refuge once, and then redirect your attention to the recitation — not to the thought. Every time you re-engage the thought to push it away again, you are engaging it. The instruction is to dismiss and redirect, not to fight. If the thought is extremely persistent and is causing significant distress or disrupting daily life, consider speaking to a Muslim therapist who specialises in OCD — this level of waswas responds well to professional support.

Are these thoughts a sign I have weak faith?

The opposite. The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ about thoughts too terrible to speak of, and he told them it was a sign of faith. Weak faith does not feel horror at blasphemy — it is indifferent to it. Your horror is itself the proof of your belief. These thoughts belong to Shaitan. Your response to them belongs to you. And your response is faith.

Does Surah An-Nas actually help?

Yes — and this is not just spiritual reassurance. The surah is specifically revealed as a protection against waswas. The Prophet ﷺ instructed reciting Al-Mu'awwidhatayn (Al-Falaq and An-Nas) morning and evening for protection. Scholars have specifically noted their effect on waswas. Recite them with understanding — read the translation — and use them as your daily armour, not just your emergency response.

The Thought Is Not You

You did not choose that thought. You did not want it. You were horrified by it. That horror is your faith defending itself.

The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ — people who gave everything for Islam, who witnessed revelation, who carried this deen to the world — felt the same thing. They came to him in distress. And he told them it was a sign of faith.

You are in their company in this experience. Not in the company of people whose faith is failing — in the company of people whose faith is real enough to be attacked.

Keep praying. Say the refuge. Finish your salah. Come back tomorrow.

The waswas retreats from people who refuse to stop.
